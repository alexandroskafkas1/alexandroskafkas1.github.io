Recitation 1: Vectors, Dot & Cross Products, Lines and Planes

Problem 1: Vectors, Unit Vectors, and Velocity

Let \(A = (1, -2, 4)\) and \(B = (3, 2, 0)\) be two points in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

  1. Find the component form and the magnitude of the displacement vector \(\overrightarrow{AB}\).
  2. Find a vector \(\vec{w}\) of length \(6\) pointing in the direction opposite to \(\overrightarrow{AB}\).
  3. If a particle travels from \(A\) to \(B\) along a straight path at a constant speed in \(4\) seconds, find its velocity vector \(\vec{v}\) and its speed.
View Solution
  1. The displacement vector is: $$\overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 3 - 1,\; 2 - (-2),\; 0 - 4 \rangle = \langle 2, 4, -4 \rangle$$ Its magnitude is: $$\|\overrightarrow{AB}\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 4^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 16 + 16} = \sqrt{36} = 6$$
  2. The unit vector in the direction of \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) is: $$\hat{u} = \frac{\overrightarrow{AB}}{\|\overrightarrow{AB}\|} = \frac{1}{6}\langle 2, 4, -4 \rangle = \left\langle \frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, -\frac{2}{3} \right\rangle$$ A vector of magnitude \(6\) pointing in the opposite direction is: $$\vec{w} = -6\hat{u} = -6\left\langle \frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, -\frac{2}{3} \right\rangle = \langle -2, -4, 4 \rangle$$
  3. The velocity vector is displacement divided by elapsed time: $$\vec{v} = \frac{\overrightarrow{AB}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\langle 2, 4, -4 \rangle}{4} = \left\langle \frac{1}{2}, 1, -1 \right\rangle$$ The speed is the magnitude of velocity: $$\|\vec{v}\| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4} + 1 + 1} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{4}} = \frac{3}{2}\text{ units/s}$$

Problem 2: Dot Product, Projections, and Work

Let \(\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, -1, 2 \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 1, 2, -2 \rangle\).

  1. Compute the dot product \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}\) and determine the angle \(\theta\) between the two vectors.
  2. Find the scalar projection \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\) and the vector projection \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\).
  3. Find the work \(W\) done by the constant force \(\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{u}\) in moving an object along the displacement vector \(\mathbf{d} = \langle 3, 0, 4 \rangle\) meters (assuming force is in newtons).
View Solution
  1. Compute the dot product and individual magnitudes: $$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (2)(1) + (-1)(2) + (2)(-2) = 2 - 2 - 4 = -4$$ $$\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2 + 2^2} = 3, \quad \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + (-2)^2} = 3$$ The angle \(\theta\) satisfies: $$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|} = \frac{-4}{(3)(3)} = -\frac{4}{9} \implies \theta = \cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{4}{9}\right) \approx 116.4^\circ$$
  2. The scalar projection is: $$\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|} = \frac{-4}{3}$$ The vector projection is: $$\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^2}\right)\mathbf{v} = \frac{-4}{9}\langle 1, 2, -2 \rangle = \left\langle -\frac{4}{9}, -\frac{8}{9}, \frac{8}{9} \right\rangle$$
  3. The work done is: $$W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = \langle 2, -1, 2 \rangle \cdot \langle 3, 0, 4 \rangle = (2)(3) + (-1)(0) + (2)(4) = 6 + 0 + 8 = 14\text{ J}$$

Problem 3: Cross Product, Area, and Torque

Consider the three points \(P(1, 0, 1)\), \(Q(2, 3, 0)\), and \(R(0, 2, 4)\).

  1. Find the cross product \(\overrightarrow{PQ} \times \overrightarrow{PR}\).
  2. Compute the area of the triangle \(\triangle PQR\).
  3. A force \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 2, -1, 5 \rangle\text{ N}\) is applied at point \(Q\) relative to a pivot located at \(P\). Find the torque vector \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) acting about \(P\).
View Solution
  1. Determine displacement vectors: $$\overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle 2 - 1, 3 - 0, 0 - 1 \rangle = \langle 1, 3, -1 \rangle, \quad \overrightarrow{PR} = \langle 0 - 1, 2 - 0, 4 - 1 \rangle = \langle -1, 2, 3 \rangle$$ Computing the determinant: $$\begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{PQ} \times \overrightarrow{PR} &= \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 1 & 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & 3 \end{vmatrix} \\ &= \mathbf{i}(9 - (-2)) - \mathbf{j}(3 - 1) + \mathbf{k}(2 - (-3)) = \langle 11, -2, 5 \rangle \end{aligned}$$
  2. The area of \(\triangle PQR\) is half the cross product magnitude: $$\|\overrightarrow{PQ} \times \overrightarrow{PR}\| = \sqrt{11^2 + (-2)^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{150} = 5\sqrt{6}$$ $$\text{Area}(\triangle PQR) = \frac{1}{2}(5\sqrt{6}) = \frac{5\sqrt{6}}{2}$$
  3. With position vector \(\mathbf{r} = \overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle 1, 3, -1 \rangle\): $$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\tau} &= \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 1 & 3 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 & 5 \end{vmatrix} \\ &= \mathbf{i}(15 - 1) - \mathbf{j}(5 - (-2)) + \mathbf{k}(-1 - 6) = \langle 14, -7, -7 \rangle\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} \end{aligned}$$

Problem 4: Equations of Lines & Planes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

  1. Find the scalar equation of the plane \(\mathcal{P}\) passing through the points \(P(1, 0, 1)\), \(Q(2, 3, 0)\), and \(R(0, 2, 4)\).
  2. Find the parametric equations of the line \(L\) passing through \(S(12, -2, 6)\) that is perpendicular to the plane \(\mathcal{P}\).
  3. Find the point where the line \(L\) intersects the plane \(\mathcal{P}\).
View Solution
  1. Using normal \(\vec{n} = \langle 11, -2, 5 \rangle\) from Problem 3 and point \(P(1, 0, 1)\): $$11(x - 1) - 2(y - 0) + 5(z - 1) = 0 \implies 11x - 2y + 5z = 16$$
  2. Since \(L\) is perpendicular to \(\mathcal{P}\), its direction vector is \(\vec{n} = \langle 11, -2, 5 \rangle\). Passing through \(S(12, -2, 6)\): $$x(t) = 12 + 11t, \quad y(t) = -2 - 2t, \quad z(t) = 6 + 5t, \quad t \in \mathbb{R}$$
  3. Substitute the parametric equations into the plane's scalar equation: $$11(12 + 11t) - 2(-2 - 2t) + 5(6 + 5t) = 16$$ $$(132 + 121t) + (4 + 4t) + (30 + 25t) = 16 \implies 150t + 166 = 16 \implies t = -1$$ Evaluating at \(t = -1\): $$x = 12 + 11(-1) = 1, \quad y = -2 - 2(-1) = 0, \quad z = 6 + 5(-1) = 1$$ The line intersects the plane at \((1, 0, 1)\) (point \(P\)).

Recitation 2: Quadric Surfaces, Vector Functions & Space Curves

Problem 1: Quadric Surface Analysis & Traces

Study the quadric surface given by the equation: $$4x^2 - y^2 + z^2 = 4$$

  1. Write the equation in standard form and identify the type of quadric surface.
  2. Find all coordinate intercepts (if any).
  3. Find equations for the traces in the three coordinate planes (\(xy\), \(xz\), and \(yz\)) and identify each curve.
View Solution
  1. Dividing both sides by \(4\): $$x^2 - \frac{y^2}{4} + \frac{z^2}{4} = 1$$ This is a hyperboloid of one sheet opening along the \(y\)-axis (due to the negative sign on \(y^2\)).
  2. Coordinate Intercepts:
    • \(x\)-intercepts (\(y = z = 0\)): \(x^2 = 1 \implies x = \pm 1 \implies (\pm 1, 0, 0)\)
    • \(y\)-intercepts (\(x = z = 0\)): \(-\frac{y^2}{4} = 1\) (no real solutions, so no \(y\)-intercepts)
    • \(z\)-intercepts (\(x = y = 0\)): \(\frac{z^2}{4} = 1 \implies z = \pm 2 \implies (0, 0, \pm 2)\)
  3. Coordinate Traces:
    • \(xy\)-trace (\(z=0\)): \(x^2 - \frac{y^2}{4} = 1\) (a hyperbola opening along the \(x\)-axis)
    • \(yz\)-trace (\(x=0\)): \(\frac{z^2}{4} - \frac{y^2}{4} = 1\) (a hyperbola opening along the \(z\)-axis)
    • \(xz\)-trace (\(y=0\)): \(x^2 + \frac{z^2}{4} = 1\) (an ellipse centered at the origin)

Problem 2: Domain of a Vector-Valued Function

Find the domain of the vector-valued function: $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle \sqrt{9-t^2}, \; \frac{\sin(2t)}{t}, \; \ln(t+1) \right\rangle$$ Express your answer in interval notation.

View Solution

The domain of \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) is the intersection of the domains of its component functions:

  • \(\sqrt{9-t^2} \implies 9 - t^2 \ge 0 \implies t \in [-3, 3]\)
  • \(\dfrac{\sin(2t)}{t} \implies t \neq 0 \implies t \in (-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\)
  • \(\ln(t+1) \implies t + 1 > 0 \implies t \in (-1, \infty)\)

Intersecting these intervals yields: $$\text{Domain} = [-3, 3] \cap \big((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\big) \cap (-1, \infty) = \mathbf{(-1, 0) \cup (0, 3]}$$

Problem 3: Intersection Curve of Surfaces

Find a vector-valued function \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) that parametrizes the curve of intersection between the circular cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 = 16\) and the plane \(2x - y + z = 4\). Include the domain for parameter \(t\).

View Solution

The cylinder projects onto a circle of radius \(4\) in the \(xy\)-plane. Parametrize \(x\) and \(y\) using polar form: $$x(t) = 4\cos t, \quad y(t) = 4\sin t, \qquad t \in [0, 2\pi)$$

Substitute these expressions into the plane equation solving for \(z\): $$z(t) = 4 - 2x + y = 4 - 2(4\cos t) + 4\sin t = 4 - 8\cos t + 4\sin t$$

Thus, the parametrization is: $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{\langle 4\cos t, \; 4\sin t, \; 4 - 8\cos t + 4\sin t \rangle}, \qquad t \in [0, 2\pi)$$

Problem 4: Velocity, Unit Tangent Vector, & Tangent Lines

Consider the space curve given by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t^2, \; 2t, \; \ln t \rangle\) for \(t > 0\).

  1. Find the velocity vector \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) and evaluate the speed \(\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\) at \(t = 1\).
  2. Find the unit tangent vector \(\mathbf{T}(t)\) at \(t = 1\).
  3. Find parametric equations for the tangent line to the curve at \(t = 1\).
View Solution
  1. Differentiate component-wise: $$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \left\langle 2t, \; 2, \; \frac{1}{t} \right\rangle \implies \mathbf{r}'(1) = \langle 2, 2, 1 \rangle$$ The speed is: $$\|\mathbf{r}'(1)\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{9} = \mathbf{3}$$
  2. The unit tangent vector is: $$\mathbf{T}(1) = \frac{\mathbf{r}'(1)}{\|\mathbf{r}'(1)\|} = \frac{\langle 2, 2, 1 \rangle}{3} = \mathbf{\left\langle \frac{2}{3}, \; \frac{2}{3}, \; \frac{1}{3} \right\rangle}$$
  3. The point on the curve at \(t = 1\) is \(\mathbf{r}(1) = \langle 1^2, 2(1), \ln 1 \rangle = (1, 2, 0)\). Using direction vector \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 2, 2, 1 \rangle\) and parametter \(s \in \mathbb{R}\): $$x(s) = 1 + 2s, \quad y(s) = 2 + 2s, \quad z(s) = s$$

Problem 5: Initial Value Kinematics

A particle moves with acceleration \(\mathbf{a}(t) = \langle -\cos t, \; -\sin t, \; 6t \rangle\) for \(t \ge 0\). Its initial velocity is \(\mathbf{v}(0) = \langle 0, 2, 1 \rangle\) and initial position is \(\mathbf{r}(0) = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle\). Find the velocity vector \(\mathbf{v}(t)\) and position vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) for all \(t \ge 0\).

View Solution
  1. Velocity: Integrating acceleration gives: $$\mathbf{v}(t) = \int \mathbf{a}(t)\,dt = \langle -\sin t, \; \cos t, \; 3t^2 \rangle + \mathbf{C}_1$$ Using \(\mathbf{v}(0) = \langle 0, 1, 0 \rangle + \mathbf{C}_1 = \langle 0, 2, 1 \rangle \implies \mathbf{C}_1 = \langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle\): $$\mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{\langle -\sin t, \; 1 + \cos t, \; 3t^2 + 1 \rangle}$$
  2. Position: Integrating velocity gives: $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \int \mathbf{v}(t)\,dt = \langle \cos t, \; t + \sin t, \; t^3 + t \rangle + \mathbf{C}_2$$ Using \(\mathbf{r}(0) = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle + \mathbf{C}_2 = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle \implies \mathbf{C}_2 = \langle 0, 0, 0 \rangle\): $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{\langle \cos t, \; t + \sin t, \; t^3 + t \rangle}$$

Recitation 3: Arc Length, Line Integrals & Motion in Space

Problem 1: Arc Length of a Space Curve

Consider the space curve parameterized by $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle 3t, \; 3t^2, \; 2t^3 \right\rangle, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2$$

  1. Compute the velocity vector \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) and show that the speed simplifies to \(\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = 3(1 + 2t^2)\).
  2. Calculate the total arc length \(L\) of the curve over the intervral \([0, 2]\).
View Solution
  1. Differentiating each component with respect to \(t\): $$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\left\langle 3t, \; 3t^2, \; 2t^3 \right\rangle = \langle 3, \; 6t, \; 6t^2 \rangle$$ The speed is the magnitude of the velocity vector: $$\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| &= \sqrt{3^2 + (6t)^2 + (6t^2)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 36t^2 + 36t^4} \\ &= \sqrt{9\left(1 + 4t^2 + 4t^4\right)} = 3\sqrt{\left(1 + 2t^2\right)^2} = \mathbf{3\left(1 + 2t^2\right) = 3 + 6t^2} \end{aligned}$$
  2. Using the arc length formula: $$\begin{aligned} L &= \int_0^2 \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt = \int_0^2 \left(3 + 6t^2\right)\,dt \\ &= \left[ 3t + 2t^3 \right]_0^2 = \left(3(2) + 2(2)^3\right) - 0 = 6 + 16 = \mathbf{22} \end{aligned}$$

Problem 2: Scalar Line Integral & Total Mass of a Wire

A thin metallic wire is coiled along the circular helix $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 4\cos t, \; 4\sin t, \; 3t \rangle, \qquad 0 \le t \le \pi$$

  1. Find the differential arc length element \(ds = \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt\).
  2. If the linear mass density of the wire at any point is \(\rho(x, y, z) = x + y + z\), evaluate the scalar line integral \(M = \int_C \rho(x, y, z)\,ds\) to find the total mass of the wire.
View Solution
  1. Compute the velocity vector: $$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -4\sin t, \; 4\cos t, \; 3 \rangle$$ The speed along the helix is: $$\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = \sqrt{(-4\sin t)^2 + (4\cos t)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{16(\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t) + 9} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$$ Thus, the differential arc length element is: $$ds = \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt = \mathbf{5\,dt}$$
  2. Evaluate the density function along the parameterized path: $$\rho(\mathbf{r}(t)) = x(t) + y(t) + z(t) = 4\cos t + 4\sin t + 3t$$ Substitute into the scalar line integral formula: $$\begin{aligned} M &= \int_0^\pi \rho(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt = \int_0^\pi (4\cos t + 4\sin t + 3t)(5)\,dt \\ &= 5 \left[ 4\sin t - 4\cos t + \frac{3}{2}t^2 \right]_0^\pi \\ &= 5 \left[ \left(4\sin\pi - 4\cos\pi + \frac{3\pi^2}{2}\right) - \left(4\sin 0 - 4\cos 0 + 0\right) \right] \\ &= 5 \left[ \left(0 - 4(-1) + \frac{3\pi^2}{2}\right) - (0 - 4(1)) \right] \\ &= 5 \left( 4 + \frac{3\pi^2}{2} + 4 \right) = 5\left(8 + \frac{3\pi^2}{2}\right) = \mathbf{40 + \frac{15\pi^2}{2}} \end{aligned}$$

Problem 3: Frenet–Serret Frame (T, N, B)

Consider the circular helix \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 3\cos t, \; 3\sin t, \; 4t \rangle\).

  1. Find the unit tangent vector \(\mathbf{T}(t)\) for any parameter value \(t\).
  2. Compute the principal unit normal vector \(\mathbf{N}(t) = \dfrac{\mathbf{T}'(t)}{\|\mathbf{T}'(t)\|}\).
  3. At \(t = 0\), evaluate \(\mathbf{T}(0)\) and \(\mathbf{N}(0)\), and compute the binormal vector \(\mathbf{B}(0) = \mathbf{T}(0) \times \mathbf{N}(0)\).
View Solution
  1. First compute the velocity vector and speed: $$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -3\sin t, \; 3\cos t, \; 4 \rangle$$ $$\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = \sqrt{(-3\sin t)^2 + (3\cos t)^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9(\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t) + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$ The unit tangent vector is: $$\mathbf{T}(t) = \frac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|} = \mathbf{\left\langle -\frac{3}{5}\sin t, \; \frac{3}{5}\cos t, \; \frac{4}{5} \right\rangle}$$
  2. Differrentiating \(\mathbf{T}(t)\) with respect to \(t\): $$\mathbf{T}'(t) = \left\langle -\frac{3}{5}\cos t, \; -\frac{3}{5}\sin t, \; 0 \right\rangle$$ Its magnitude is: $$\|\mathbf{T}'(t)\| = \sqrt{\left(-\frac{3}{5}\cos t\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{3}{5}\sin t\right)^2 + 0^2} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{25}(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t)} = \frac{3}{5}$$ Therefore, the principal unit normal vector is: $$\mathbf{N}(t) = \frac{\mathbf{T}'(t)}{\|\mathbf{T}'(t)\|} = \frac{\left\langle -\frac{3}{5}\cos t, \; -\frac{3}{5}\sin t, \; 0 \right\rangle}{\frac{3}{5}} = \mathbf{\langle -\cos t, \; -\sin t, \; 0 \rangle}$$ (Note: \(\mathbf{N}(t)\) points horizontally directly inward toward the central axis of the helix.)
  3. Evaluating at \(t = 0\): $$\mathbf{T}(0) = \left\langle 0, \; \frac{3}{5}, \; \frac{4}{5} \right\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{N}(0) = \langle -1, \; 0, \; 0 \rangle$$ The binormal vector at \(t = 0\) is: $$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{B}(0) &= \mathbf{T}(0) \times \mathbf{N}(0) = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 0 & \frac{3}{5} & \frac{4}{5} \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{vmatrix} \\ &= \mathbf{i}\left[\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)(0) - \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)(0)\right] - \mathbf{j}\left[(0)(0) - \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)(-1)\right] + \mathbf{k}\left[(0)(0) - \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)(-1)\right] \\ &= \mathbf{\left\langle 0, \; -\frac{4}{5}, \; \frac{3}{5} \right\rangle} \end{aligned}$$

Problem 4: 3D Projectile Motion & Impact Analysis

An engineering projectile is launched from the origin \((0, 0, 0)\) with an initial velocity of $$\mathbf{v}(0) = \langle 30, \; 40, \; 50 \rangle \quad \text{(in m/s)}$$ Assuming downward gravitational acceleration \(\mathbf{a}(t) = \langle 0, 0, -10 \rangle\text{ m/s}^2\) and neglecting air resistance:

  1. Find the velocity vector \(\mathbf{v}(t)\) and position vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) for all \(t \ge 0\).
  2. Find the time \(T\) when the projectile impacts the ground (\(z = 0\)) and determine the maximum height \(H\) reached during flight.
  3. Determine the projectile's landing location, its horizontal range from the origin, and its speed at impact.
View Solution
  1. Integrating acceleration \(\mathbf{a}(t) = \langle 0, 0, -10 \rangle\): $$\mathbf{v}(t) = \int \mathbf{a}(t)\,dt = \langle 0, 0, -10t \rangle + \mathbf{C}_1$$ Using initial velocity \(\mathbf{v}(0) = \langle 30, 40, 50 \rangle\), we get \(\mathbf{C}_1 = \langle 30, 40, 50 \rangle\): $$\mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{\langle 30, \; 40, \; 50 - 10t \rangle}$$ Integrating velocity with initial position \(\mathbf{r}(0) = \langle 0, 0, 0 \rangle\): $$\mathbf{r}(t) = \int \mathbf{v}(t)\,dt = \mathbf{\left\langle 30t, \; 40t, \; 50t - 5t^2 \right\rangle}$$
  2. Setting the vertical component to zero to find the flight time \(T\): $$z(T) = 50T - 5T^2 = 0 \implies 5T(10 - T) = 0 \implies \mathbf{T = 10\text{ s}}$$ Maximum height occurs when vertical velocity is zero: $$v_z(t) = 50 - 10t = 0 \implies t = 5\text{ s}$$ Evaluating vertical position at \(t = 5\): $$H = z(5) = 50(5) - 5(5)^2 = 250 - 125 = \mathbf{125\text{ m}}$$
  3. Substituting \(T = 10\text{ s}\) into \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) gives the landing coordinates: $$\mathbf{r}(10) = \langle 30(10), \; 40(10), \; 0 \rangle = \mathbf{(300, \; 400, \; 0)}$$ The horizontal range from the launch point is: $$R = \sqrt{x(10)^2 + y(10)^2} = \sqrt{300^2 + 400^2} = \sqrt{90{,}000 + 160{,}000} = \mathbf{500\text{ m}}$$ The velocity at impact is \(\mathbf{v}(10) = \langle 30, \; 40, \; 50 - 10(10) \rangle = \langle 30, \; 40, \; -50 \rangle\text{ m/s}\), giving an impact speed of: $$\|\mathbf{v}(10)\| = \sqrt{30^2 + 40^2 + (-50)^2} = \sqrt{5000} = \mathbf{50\sqrt{2} \approx 70.71\text{ m/s}}$$

Recitation 4: Multivariable Functions, Partial Derivatives & The Chain Rule

Problem 1: Domain, Range, and Level Curves

Consider the multivariable function:

$$f(x, y) = \sqrt{16 - 4x^2 - y^2}$$
  1. Find the domain and range of \(f(x,y)\), and describe the geometric shape of the domain in the \(xy\)-plane.
  2. Find the equations of the level curves \(f(x, y) = c\) for \(c = 0\), \(c = 2\sqrt{3}\), and \(c = 4\). Identify each resulting curve.
  3. Identify the surface given by the graph \(z = f(x, y)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
View Solution
  1. The expression under the radical must be nonnegative: $$16 - 4x^2 - y^2 \ge 0 \implies 4x^2 + y^2 \le 16 \implies \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{16} \le 1.$$ The domain is the closed elliptical disk centered at \((0,0)\) with semi-minor axis \(2\) and semi-major axis \(4\). Since \(0 \le 16 - 4x^2 - y^2 \le 16\), the range is \([0, 4]\).
  2. Setting \(f(x, y) = c\) gives \(4x^2 + y^2 = 16 - c^2\):
    • For \(c = 0\): \(\dfrac{x^2}{4} + \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1\) (ellipse with semi-axes \(2\) and \(4\)).
    • For \(c = 2\sqrt{3}\): \(4x^2 + y^2 = 16 - 12 = 4 \implies x^2 + \dfrac{y^2}{4} = 1\) (ellipse with semi-axes \(1\) and \(2\)).
    • For \(c = 4\): \(4x^2 + y^2 = 0 \implies (x, y) = (0, 0)\) (single point at the origin).
  3. Setting \(z = \sqrt{16 - 4x^2 - y^2}\) with \(z \ge 0\) gives: $$\frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{16} + \frac{z^2}{16} = 1 \quad (z \ge 0),$$ which is the upper hemisphere of an ellipsoid centered at the origin.

Problem 2: Partial Derivatives & Clairaut's Theorem

Consider the function \(f(x, y) = x^3 \ln y + y\,e^{2x}\).

  1. State the natural domain of \(f(x, y)\).
  2. Compute the first-order partial derivatives \(f_x(x, y)\) and \(f_y(x, y)\).
  3. Compute all second-order partial derivatives \(f_{xx}\), \(f_{yy}\), \(f_{xy}\), and \(f_{yx}\), and verify that Clairaut's Theorem holds.
  4. Evaluate \(f_{xy}(1, 1)\).
View Solution
  1. The logarithm requires \(y > 0\), so the domain is: $$D = \{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2 \mid y > 0\} = \mathbb{R} \times (0, \infty).$$
  2. Differentiating holding the opposite variable constant: $$f_x(x, y) = 3x^2 \ln y + 2y e^{2x}, \qquad f_y(x, y) = \frac{x^3}{y} + e^{2x}.$$
  3. Differentiating the first partials: $$\begin{aligned} f_{xx}(x, y) &= 6x \ln y + 4y e^{2x}, \qquad f_{yy}(x, y) = -\frac{x^3}{y^2}, \\ f_{xy}(x, y) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(f_x) = \frac{3x^2}{y} + 2e^{2x}, \qquad f_{yx}(x, y) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(f_y) = \frac{3x^2}{y} + 2e^{2x}. \end{aligned}$$ Since both mixed partials are continuous and equal on the entire domain, \(f_{xy} = f_{yx}\) (Clairaut's Theorem verified).
  4. At \((1, 1)\): $$f_{xy}(1, 1) = \frac{3(1)^2}{1} + 2e^{2(1)} = 3 + 2e^2.$$

Problem 3: Multivariable Chain Rule

Let \(z = x^2 y - 3xy^2\), where \(x(u, v) = u^2 - v^2\) and \(y(u, v) = 2uv\).

  1. State the Chain Rule expressions for \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial u}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial v}\).
  2. Find the values of \(x\) and \(y\) when \((u, v) = (1, 1)\).
  3. Evaluate \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial u}\) at the point \((u, v) = (1, 1)\).
View Solution
  1. By the multivariable Chain Rule: $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial v} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}.$$
  2. At \((u, v) = (1, 1)\): $$x(1, 1) = 1^2 - 1^2 = 0, \qquad y(1, 1) = 2(1)(1) = 2.$$
  3. Evaluating the partials at \((x, y) = (0, 2)\) and \((u, v) = (1, 1)\): $$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} &= 2xy - 3y^2 \implies \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}(0, 2) = 0 - 3(2^2) = -12, \\ \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} &= x^2 - 6xy \implies \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}(0, 2) = 0 - 0 = 0, \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} &= 2u \implies \frac{\partial x}{\partial u}(1, 1) = 2, \qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} = 2v \implies \frac{\partial y}{\partial u}(1, 1) = 2. \end{aligned}$$ Substituting into the Chain Rule: $$\left.\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\right|_{(1,1)} = (-12)(2) + (0)(2) = -24.$$

Problem 4: Implicit Differentiation & Rates of Change

  1. The relation \(x^2 y + y z^3 - 2xz = 0\) defines \(z\) implicitly as a function of \(x\) and \(y\). Use the total differential to derive formulas for \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\).
  2. The electrical power across a resistor is \(P = I^2 R\). At a given instant, \(I = 5\text{ A}\) and increases at \(\dfrac{dI}{dt} = 0.2\text{ A/s}\), while \(R = 40\ \Omega\) and increases at \(\dfrac{dR}{dt} = 0.5\ \Omega/\text{s}\). Compute \(\dfrac{dP}{dt}\) and identify which parameter contributes more to the power surge.
View Solution
  1. Let \(F(x, y, z) = x^2 y + y z^3 - 2xz = 0\). The partial derivatives are: $$F_x = 2xy - 2z, \qquad F_y = x^2 + z^3, \qquad F_z = 3yz^2 - 2x.$$ Using the implicit differentiation identities: $$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} &= -\frac{F_x}{F_z} = -\frac{2xy - 2z}{3yz^2 - 2x} = \frac{2z - 2xy}{3yz^2 - 2x}, \\ \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} &= -\frac{F_y}{F_z} = -\frac{x^2 + z^3}{3yz^2 - 2x}. \end{aligned}$$
  2. Applying the Chain Rule to \(P(I, R)\): $$\frac{dP}{dt} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial I}\frac{dI}{dt} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial R}\frac{dR}{dt} = (2IR)\frac{dI}{dt} + (I^2)\frac{dR}{dt}.$$ Substituting the values: $$\frac{dP}{dt} = 2(5)(40)(0.2) + (5^2)(0.5) = 80 + 12.5 = 92.5\text{ W/s}.$$ The current contributes \(80\text{ W/s}\) while the resistance contributes \(12.5\text{ W/s}\). Thus, the current fluctuation dominates because power scales quadratically with current (\(I^2\)).
